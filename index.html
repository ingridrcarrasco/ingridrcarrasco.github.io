Studying worlds through physics, landscapes, and light.

I am a planetary scientist entering graduate study in Earth sciences and geophysics. My academic interests center on how planets and moons evolve over time, especially the physical processes that influence atmospheres, surfaces, interiors, and habitability.

My background includes exoplanet habitability modeling, icy world research, spectroscopy, astronomical instrumentation, telescope operation, detector testing, student research leadership, and public science outreach. I am interested in work that connects planetary questions with geophysical methods and hands-on scientific systems.

Current Direction

This website documents my academic and professional work as I transition into my M.S. in Earth Sciences with a concentration in Geophysics at the University of Oregon. I plan to use this space to share research materials, selected presentations, outreach work, CV updates, and future publications.

I am especially interested in the overlap between planetary science and geophysics: how physical environments evolve, how internal and external energy sources shape worlds, and how Earth-based methods can help us understand other planetary bodies.

Research Areas

Planetary Habitability

I study habitability as a changing process, including how atmospheres, stellar radiation, climate feedbacks, and internal energy can affect whether planetary environments become, remain, or return to being habitable.

Geophysics

My graduate direction focuses on Earth sciences and geophysics, with interests in planetary materials, interiors, surface processes, icy worlds, and the physical systems that shape dynamic environments.

Instrumentation

I have hands-on experience with spectroscopy, telescope systems, optomechanical alignment, semiconductor chip testing, astronomical observing, and scientific tools that turn measurements into data.

Selected Work

My research experience includes modeling the long-term habitability of icy satellite environments using ExoPlaSim, testing semiconductor chips for the CERN CMS Inner Tracker upgrade, and building a complete spectrograph system through AstroTech at UC Berkeley.

These experiences have shaped how I think about science: as both a theoretical and hands-on process. I enjoy research that requires asking big planetary questions while also understanding the instruments, materials, and methods that make those questions measurable.
